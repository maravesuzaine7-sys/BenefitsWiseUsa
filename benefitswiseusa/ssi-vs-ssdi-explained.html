SSI vs SSDI: What's the Difference and Which One Do You Qualify For?

📅 Updated January 2026⏱ 7 min read ✓ 2026 figures verified
The simple difference: SSI (Supplemental Security Income) is based on financial need — it does not matter if you have ever worked. SSDI (Social Security Disability Insurance) is based on your work history — you must have paid Social Security taxes to qualify. Both require a qualifying disability.

Side-by-side comparison

Need-based

SSI — Supplemental Security Income

  • Based on financial need, not work history
  • Anyone can apply — even if never worked
  • Must have very low income and assets
  • Asset limit: $2,000 (single) / $3,000 (couple)
  • Max benefit 2026: $967/month (single)
  • Automatically qualifies you for Medicaid in most states
  • For aged (65+), blind, or disabled people

Work-based

SSDI — Social Security Disability Insurance

  • Based on your work and Social Security tax history
  • Must have earned enough "work credits"
  • No asset or savings limit
  • Benefit amount varies based on earnings history
  • Average benefit 2026: $1,537/month
  • Qualifies for Medicare after 24 months on SSDI
  • Only for people with qualifying disabilities

What is SSI?

SSI stands for Supplemental Security Income. It is a federal program run by the Social Security Administration (SSA) that pays monthly benefits to people who are aged (65 or older), blind, or disabled and who have very limited income and resources.

SSI is a needs-based program — meaning your financial situation is the main factor. You do not need to have ever worked or paid Social Security taxes to receive SSI. It is designed as a safety net for people who have little to no income and few assets.

Some states add a supplemental payment on top of the federal SSI amount. Your actual payment may be higher depending on where you live.

SSI asset limit: To qualify for SSI, you must have less than $2,000 in countable resources if you are single, or $3,000 if you are a couple. Your home, one car, and certain other items do not count toward this limit.

What is SSDI?

SSDI stands for Social Security Disability Insurance. It is an insurance program that pays monthly benefits to people who have a qualifying disability and have worked long enough and recently enough to be "insured" under Social Security.

Think of SSDI like an insurance policy you paid into through your payroll taxes. If you become disabled and can no longer work, SSDI pays you a monthly benefit based on your lifetime earnings history — similar to how a retirement benefit works, but triggered by disability instead of age.

How much does SSDI pay?

Unlike SSI, SSDI payments are not a fixed amount. Your benefit is calculated based on your average lifetime earnings that were subject to Social Security taxes. The more you earned over your working life, the higher your SSDI benefit.

How to qualify — the disability requirement

Both SSI and SSDI require you to have a disability that meets the Social Security Administration's definition. The SSA defines disability as a physical or mental condition that:

  • Prevents you from doing any "substantial gainful activity" (basically, any meaningful paid work)
  • Has lasted or is expected to last at least 12 months, or is expected to result in death

The SSA has a list of medical conditions (called the "Blue Book") that automatically qualify. If your condition is not on the list, you can still qualify if you can show your condition is as severe as a listed one.

Can you receive both SSI and SSDI at the same time?

Yes — this is called receiving "concurrent benefits." It happens when your SSDI payment is low enough that you still qualify financially for SSI. In this case, SSI tops up your SSDI to ensure you receive at least the minimum monthly amount.

How to apply for SSI or SSDI

1

Apply online or by phone

For SSDI, apply at ssa.gov/applyfordisability or call the SSA at 1-800-772-1213. For SSI, you must call the SSA or visit a local Social Security office — SSI cannot be applied for fully online.

2

Gather your documents

You will need your Social Security number, birth certificate, medical records showing your disability, work history for the past 15 years, and bank account information. For SSI, you will also need proof of income and resources.

3

Expect a wait — and be prepared to appeal

Initial decisions take 3–6 months on average. About 65% of initial applications are denied. If you are denied, appeal immediately — do not start a new application. Most approvals happen at the appeal stage.

4

Consider getting help

Disability attorneys work on contingency — they only get paid if you win, and their fee is capped by law. For complex cases, getting legal representation significantly improves your chances of approval.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to get approved?

Initial decisions typically take 3–6 months. If denied and you appeal, the process can take 1–2 years in total. Once approved for SSDI, there is also a 5-month waiting period before your first payment begins.

What happens to my SSDI when I turn 65?

Your SSDI automatically converts to Social Security retirement benefits at full retirement age (67 for most people). The monthly amount stays the same — nothing changes for you practically.

Can I work while receiving SSI or SSDI?

You can do limited work. For SSDI, you can earn up to $1,550/month in 2026 (the Substantial Gainful Activity limit) without losing benefits. SSI has a different formula — the first $85/month you earn does not affect your benefit, and after that your SSI is reduced by $1 for every $2 you earn.
